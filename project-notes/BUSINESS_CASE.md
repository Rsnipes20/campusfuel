# CampusFuel business case — student writing worksheet

This is a planning worksheet, not a finished submission. Replace prompts with your own reasoning and wording. Aim for about 1,500 words; text beyond 1,800 is not graded. The optional ideas below are hypotheses to evaluate, not established competitive advantages.

## 1. The idea (about 200 words)
- Which university students are the specific customer? Narrow the group beyond “everyone.”
- What makes tracking meals inconvenient for them? Use an example you can explain from your own experience without disclosing private health information.
- Walk through search, meal selection, servings and daily totals in this prototype.
- Why would someone choose this over another tracker? What would you need to test?
- Would a free tracker with a paid planning tier be credible? Name a proposed price, explain your reasoning and label it as an assumption. No paid features are implemented.

## 2. Where IT creates value (about 200 words)
- Identify the value-chain activities affected, such as service delivery, customer support and product development.
- Explain how automatic calculations and reusable food records change the work involved.
- Separate operational effectiveness (doing a similar task efficiently) from strategic positioning (serving a particular need differently).
- Point to specific actions on the website rather than describing IT in general.

## 3. Network effects (about 250 words)
- Does one student's private food log help another student? Explain your answer.
- The current product is a single customer-group service, not a two-sided marketplace. A large user base does not automatically create network effects.
- Explain why personal history and favourites can create switching costs without being a network effect.
- What would solve the launch problem when no one has used the tracker? Consider a useful initial food catalogue and a small pilot; these are proposals, not completed research.
- Refer to “Why it matters” and its illustrative history selector. Explain that it models accumulated personal convenience, not network growth.
- If you propose shared recipes later, explain both a possible network mechanism and moderation/quality problems. Keep proposed features separate from the current prototype.

## 4. Can it be copied? (about 450 words)
- A competitor can rebuild this website quickly. Explain why the code itself is not a moat.
- Choose two or three candidate resources, such as a trusted community, reliable specialised food records or user routines. State which exist now and which are hypothetical.
- Test EACH against the VRIN framework taught in class: valuable, rare, inimitable and non-substitutable. Confirm wording with your professor's slides. The template's “well-organized” wording differs from standard VRIN.
- For each criterion, give a reason and acknowledge failures. Do not just name the four letters.
- What would a well-funded competitor need to copy each resource? Avoid invented claims about time, ownership or exclusive partnerships.
- Which resources could strengthen over time, and what evidence would demonstrate it?
- Discuss how CSV export and using multiple apps limit switching costs. Explain why fair portability may still build trust.

## 5. Cloud and operations (about 220 words)
- Distinguish this static browser demo from a real service with accounts and synchronised data.
- Discuss SaaS for an off-the-shelf support or billing service, PaaS for an application/database platform, and IaaS for rented computing infrastructure. Decide what you would actually use and why; you do not need all three.
- Cover nutrition data, authentication, database, backups, security, support, and payments only if charging.
- Explain costs, outages, vendor dependence and data protection risks, with practical responses.

## 6. Risks and ethics (about 180 words)
- Consider privacy of food records, inaccurate nutrition information, unhelpful calorie targets and pressure to restrict eating.
- Give a concrete response to each risk, such as data minimisation, deletion/export, qualified review and neutral language.
- Explain why this demo uses fictional data and illustrative goals instead of collecting personal information.
